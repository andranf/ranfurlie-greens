Ranfurlie Golf ClubHow our course maintenance team runs on Claude, Cirrus Pro, TurfRad, TurfDog, Slack and Notion

At Ranfurlie we run the course off more than twenty systems: the irrigation central, a moisture radar on the greens mower, soil probes, weather stations, spray records, machinery telematics and the crew's job board. None of them were built to work together. Claude joins them up, so the crew starts each day with one clear picture and we deal with problems before the turf shows them.

On a normal weekday that's 124 scheduled runs across 31 jobs, and every night 683 sprinklers get a run time worked out for them one by one.

How the watering works See the dashboards

Not locked in to anyone's app.

Every tool we use is good at its own job. The trouble was always the gaps between them. The irrigation central doesn't know what the radar saw this morning. The job board doesn't know a sprinkler failed at 2am. The spray records don't know it's about to rain.

Claude reads each system the way a person would: through its website, its data feed, or a post in Slack. It checks one source against another, works out what matters, and puts the answer back where the crew already looks. When we moved the job board from taskTracker to TurfDog this month, we rewrote some instructions. Nothing else had to change.

      Spatially Claude: watering head by head.

      Most programs give every sprinkler on a green the same run time and hope the green is even. It never is. Spatially Claude works out a separate time for every head, from the moisture under that head.

      The name is a nod to Toro's Spatial Adjust. Ours runs on our Rain Bird system, with our rules, and covers the whole course: 93 heads on the greens and 590 everywhere else.

        18th green, Saturday 17 October. The back of the green read 8.8 to 10.1% on the morning scan, so those heads got the longest times. Same total water either way. The heads are placed for the diagram; the times are the real ones.

        1. Scan

          The TurfRad radar on the greens mower maps moisture across every green most mornings. A satellite pass from SkimTurf fills in the fairways the mower doesn't reach.

        2. Match

          Each patch of the scan is tied to the sprinkler that waters it, using the head positions from Cirrus Pro.

        3. Check

          The Spiio soil probes, the TDR probe and the on-course weather station check the radar, and track how much has dried out since the scan.

        4. Size

          Each head gets its own time from its own reading, its own nozzle and the day's drying. No flat sets.

        5. Write

          The times go straight into Cirrus Pro around 5pm. Claude never switches a program on. That's my call, after I've read the brief in Slack.

        6. Learn

          If I reply with a correction, it becomes a standing rule from that night on. The same goes for what it learns about the system itself.

        683sprinklers sized one at a time, every night, from the best data under each one.
        18stations is all our Rain Bird decoder interface can run at once, so every night's set is budgeted to finish before the crew arrives.
        0programs switched on by Claude. It builds them and explains them. I decide.

        Layers Cirrus Pro never had.

        Our irrigation design isn't great, and a lot of what we knew about the course lived on paper plans in the shed. Now it's going into Cirrus Pro as map layers we can switch on and off.

        We've been tracing every turf area and assigning each head to the area it actually sits in. The drainage plans and the as-built pipework are layers now too. Then we went after the sprinklers: Claude pulled the position, arc, radius and nozzle of every station, drew each head's real throw, and checked it against the traced areas. Heads putting water on bunkers or rough are coloured by priority on their own layer, so the crew can see which ones to change first.

        The 8th green. Four of its heads went to part circle in October, so they water the green and stop soaking the bunker faces. 8G3 is still full circle. Head positions are drawn for the diagram.

        Live pages the whole crew can open.

        No logins and nothing to install. Each page rebuilds through the day from what the crew posts in Slack and what the systems report, so the mechanic, the irrigation tech and the greens committee are all looking at the same thing.

        Irrigation

        Every fault the crew posts in #irrigation becomes a job with a pin on the course, greens heads first. Cirrus Pro alarms come in on their own. Parts used and parts to order are logged from photos and replies.

        Spray mixes

        Each tank becomes a step-by-step recipe on the applicator's phone. Pre-start first, then PPE, then each product in the right tank order with the amount in big numbers and the label warnings where they matter. The link goes into #spraying with the applicator tagged.

        Seeing it before it shows.

        The best day on a course is the one where nothing goes wrong because we saw it coming. The greens dashboard keeps a running list of what's due, what's about to lapse and what the weather is going to do. This is that list, read straight from the live data.

        Safety that follows itself up.

        Every safe work procedure and OHS document is on a page the crew can read on their phone and sign with a finger. Each one comes back for renewal based on how risky the job is. When someone's due, they get tagged in Slack, and they keep getting tagged until it's done.

        • Pre-starts are checked every hour from 6am. Anyone on a machine in TurfDog without a pre-start or a signed SWP for it gets a tag.
        • 18 training modules with a short knowledge check each, from site induction to lightning, heat, fire danger days and working alone, checked against current Victorian requirements.
        • Shed keys are signed out and back with a QR code on the wall. If one isn't back by Tuesday 10am, Slack hears about it.
        • The Safety Cut, a short safety message in #ohs, goes out every weekday at 9.

        What it actually takes.

        No developers and no new software contract. Every one of these started as a job that annoyed me, described to Claude in plain English.
